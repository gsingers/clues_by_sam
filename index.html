Rule 1

Every clue is true — including clues from criminals.

No clue is false, so the puzzle is a constraint satisfaction problem rather than an interrogation. A clue also never expires: one applied on move 3 still constrains the board on move 20, and hard puzzles routinely require re-reading an early clue in light of a later placement. Dim a clue only once it is exhausted, not while it is merely unhelpful.

Basics / Rules & geometry

Vocabulary and exact definitions

Each term has one in-game meaning

neighbors
All 8 surrounding cells, diagonals included.
connected
A chain of orthogonal adjacency. Along a single row or column it means no innocents between any two criminals in that line. A group of one counts as connected.
Two graphs on one board. Diagonal cells are neighbors but are never directly connected.
above / below
Anywhere in the same column. left / right: anywhere in the same row.
directly above
The one immediately adjacent cell — not the whole line.
The word "directly" converts a line clue into an adjacency clue. Check for it in every clue.
edges
Includes the corners — 14 of 20 cells. Only B2–B4 and C2–C4 are interior.
An edge clue covers 14 of 20 cells, so its value depends on how much of the board is already placed.
in between
Strictly between — excludes the two named people.
all X are Y
Carries existential import — also asserts at least one X exists.
both
Exactly 2. any is conditional and promises no existence.
odd / even
A parity clue — "an odd number of the cooks are criminals" — fixes only whether a count is odd or even, never the count itself. Its use comes later: once every member but two is resolved, the parity still required of those two forces them to differ (odd) or to match (even).
common neighbors
Neighbors of both — excludes the two referenced people themselves.
share
"Have in common." in total means the sum across the named groups.
the most
Always uniquely the most — nobody ties.
more than
Does not require the lesser side to have any at all.
if A then B
Not a biconditional. It does give you the contrapositive: not B → not A.

Two graphs, one board

Which graph each clue type uses

Counting clues use the 8-neighborhood on the left. Connectivity clues use the orthogonal graph on the right, where the relevant structures are connected regions and the innocent cells that separate them.

Board geometry

Neighbor counts by position

  • A neighbor-count clue is a lower bound on how many neighbors the cell has, so any count above 5 forces the interior — B2–B4 and C2–C4, the only cells with 8 neighbors. A low count constrains nothing by itself.
  • Where both the criminal and the innocent neighbor count are known, their sum pins the position class: 3 is a corner, 5 an edge, 8 the interior.
  • Corners are the most constrained cells: 3 neighbors and 2 orthogonal exits. Clues involving a corner reduce to a small number of cases.
  • Columns A and D consist entirely of edge cells. Rows hold 4 cells and columns hold 5, so an identical count clue constrains a row more tightly.

Advanced / Connectivity

Connectivity rules

The only clues that constrain the criminal set globally

  • Connectivity along a single line is an ordering constraint, not a count. It converts to implications: if the third cell is innocent, every cell past it is innocent. Combine that with any count clue covering the same cells.
  • The elbow rule is a choice, not a certainty. Two diagonal criminals in one connected set are joined either through an elbow or by a detour around it, and the shortest detour on a 4-wide board costs five further criminals (B2 to C3 via A2, A3, A4, B4, C4). Check the budget: with fewer than five criminals to spare, an elbow is forced.
  • Elbow constraints are "at least one", not "exactly one". Knowing that at least one of two cells is criminal fixes no relation between them, so the pair cannot later be recorded as a link.
  • A not connected clue asserts that a separating line of innocents exists. The board is 4 cells wide, so such walls are short.
  • Distance sets a lower bound on headcount. Connecting two cells requires at least |Δrow| + |Δcol| + 1 cells of that status.

Advanced / The tagging system

Three banks

Assigning the three tag colors

Each card holds a colored tag on its corner, cycled by tapping it. The board itself already records every placement, so a tag is worth spending only on a relation you have proved but cannot yet resolve — two cells that must differ, a group known to contain exactly one criminal, a cell being tested by assumption.

The palette is the constraint. Three colors are available by tap, and a meaning has to stay attached to its color for as long as the structure lives, so the colors are best treated as three assigned registers rather than free highlighting. The three banks below are those registers, listed in the order they should claim a color when the palette is tight.

A tag can record that some cells are related, never what the relation is. That belongs in the margin: a written note kept beside the board, holding each color's meaning — which cells wear it and what is known about them. A color without its margin line is unusable within a few moves. The notation section gives the margin a fixed layout, and sets out the shorthand used in the examples below.

One word of warning about that term: bank also names each half of a chain — the set of cells that turn out to be criminal, and the set that turn out to be innocent. Bank 1 holds that structure and needs two colors for it; Banks 2 and 3 are single-color roles.

Bank 1 · standing

The chain

Cells joined by same- and differ-links. However many cells it holds, a chain always has exactly two banks — so exactly two colors. Either color 1 is the criminal set and color 2 the innocent set, or the reverse.

Example"Exactly 1 of Frank and Nancy is a criminal" → A2 ⊕ A4. Tag A2 color 1, A4 color 2.
A later parity clue forces Nancy and Oscar to match → B4 takes color 2, beside Nancy.
Three cells, one chain, still two colors. Placing any one of them resolves all three.
Bank 2 · transient

Group colors

One color marks one exclusivity group with a known count. Overlapping groups are the reason to spend a color: containment, arithmetic remainder, and overlap squeeze are hard to see in clue text and immediate in color. Retire the color as soon as the group resolves.

Example"Exactly 1 singer is a criminal" — Maria, Oscar, Terry → tag D3, B4, B5 color 3.
Margin: ●3 = {D3,B4,B5}, c = 1 — color 3, those three cells, exactly one criminal among them.
Oscar is later placed criminal, which fills the quota: D3 and B5 are innocent, and color 3 is free again.
Bank 3 · scratch

Hypotheses only

One color, always the same one, cleared completely before normal play resumes. A speculative mark left among the standing banks invalidates every later deduction, and the resulting error is hard to locate.

ExampleAssume Chad is a criminal. Mark C1 and everything it forces in the scratch color only.
If that forces two criminals into a line capped at one → ⊥, so Chad is innocent: place it.
Either way — contradiction or not — clear every scratch mark before the next deduction.

Palette limits: the corner tap cycles three colors before wrapping to none. Additional colors require a long-press, and the card's second corner is a separate slot.

Same-links extend a chain as effectively as differ-links. Two cells forced to share a status take the same color.

Comparative clues

"Equal number of…" · "more … than …"

Comparative clues are neither counts nor links. They constrain the difference between two neighborhoods and do not take a color. Three methods apply:

  1. Cancel the overlap. Shared neighbors contribute equally to both sides and drop out. If each exclusive side shrinks to one cell, "equal" becomes a same-link.
  2. Compute one side exactly and substitute a pair into the other. A neighborhood containing a differ-pair has a fixed contribution from that pair, which reduces the clue to a single unknown. This method applies most often.
  3. Chain the equalities. Equality is transitive, so several "equal" clues combine into one equivalence class of counts. Fixing any member fixes all of them.

Bound the smaller side as well: a comparative against a corner (3 neighbors) is far tighter than one against an interior cell (8).

Branch-independent contribution

Using a pair before it resolves

A differ-pair contributes exactly one criminal and exactly one innocent, in both worlds. A same-pair contributes zero or two — always even. Neither needs resolving to be used.

When a pair lies inside a clue's set, subtract its guaranteed contribution and apply the clue to the remainder:

  • Parity clues — a differ-pair flips the required parity of the remainder; a same-pair leaves it untouched.
  • Comparative clues — a pair fixes one side's count outright, leaving a single unknown.
  • Exact counts — a pair consumes exactly one of the quota.

A pair is useful while unresolved. Resolving it is not a prerequisite, so apply pairs to live clues as soon as they exist.

Before assigning a color

Translate · prune · quota

  1. 01

    Translate the property into cells

    "Exactly one X has a criminal directly above them" names a category, but the constrained cells are elsewhere. Rewrite it as "exactly one criminal among {the cell directly above each X}". Tag those cells, not the category members.

  2. 02

    Prune undefined members

    Drop anyone for whom the relation is undefined: nobody in row 1 has a cell above them, nobody in row 5 has one below. Undefined members mis-size the group and corrupt the count.

  3. 03

    Check the quota

    Split into resolved and unresolved, and count what the resolved part already supplies. Only one of the three outcomes below needs a color.

A satisfied group is not a tracking problem but a set of placements. Collect them before moving on.

Applying a chain

Check these after every placement

  • Resolve one, resolve all. Prove any single tagged cell and the whole chain collapses in one pass.
  • A bank that cannot supply the count → contradiction. If a clue requires one criminal from a set holding two same-colored cells, that assignment is impossible and the other color is the criminal bank.
  • Count collision. A line known to hold zero criminals containing a color-1 cell makes color 1 the innocent bank everywhere.
  • Forced to differ, same color (or forced to match, opposite colors) → that assignment is impossible. No case analysis required.

Without tags, a stall requires re-deriving the board. With tags, it begins with the colored cells.

Tag hygiene

Retiring and reusing colors

  • Colors count banks, not cells. Three colors on three chained cells discards the structure the chain encodes. Merge them to two.
  • Don't tag what a line already shows. A count over a whole row or column needs no color. Colors are for scattered constraints.
  • Do not tag a link that resolves immediately. A same-link that resolves as soon as it is derived never needed a color.
  • A cell in two structures takes its Bank 1 color, with its group membership recorded in the margin instead of on the board.
  • Record what each color means when you assign it. An unlabeled color cannot be retired safely.

The life cycle. Groups shrink into pairs, pairs combine into a chain, and the chain collapses on a single placement. Retire a color at each step; stale tags are a common source of late-puzzle errors.

Run this scan after every placement:

  1. Did it shrink a parity remainder to two cells? → new Bank 1 link.
  2. Did it remove a member from a Bank 2 group? → recheck the quota; the group may now be a pair, or fully satisfied.
  3. Did it resolve a chain cell? → the chain collapses; clear both colors.
  4. Is any color still sitting on a resolved cell? → clear it now.

Step 4 is the one most often skipped.

Advanced / Notation & the paper ledger

The board as a structure

Symbols for cells, sets, and counts

The app records placements and tags record relations on the board. Quantifiers, implications, running counts, and discharged branches fit in neither, so they belong on paper. The notation below is chosen to be quick to write by hand.

V · C · I
The 20 cells and the two sets that partition them: C ⊎ I = V. A status is written B3 ∈ C.
c(S)
Criminals in the set S. Most clues are assertions about c of some set, and identifying which set is usually the harder half of the clue.
i(S)
|S| − c(S). Recording both values makes complement counting mechanical.
N(x) · N₄(x)
The 8-neighborhood, and the orthogonal one. G₄ is the graph the word connected lives in.
x↑ x↓ x← x→
The one cell directly adjacent in that direction. Undefined at the border: A1↑ does not exist, which is not the same as being innocent.
↑x ↓x ←x →x
The whole line beyond x. ↓B1 = {B2,B3,B4,B5}.
The side the arrow sits on distinguishes an adjacency clue from a whole-line clue. Fixing it during transcription prevents the misreading from reaching the deduction.
R1…R5 · A…D
The nine lines, for the count ledger. Rows cap at 4, columns at 5.

Typed equivalents: <= for ⊆, & for ∩, + for ⊕, = for ≡, -> for ⇒, !! for ⊥, all and some for ∀ and ∃. c(S) is unchanged.

Pairs are equations

Recording Bank 1 links

A tag color is a 2-coloring of a graph whose edges are the two link types:

  • x ⊕ y — a differ-link. Exactly one of them is criminal: c({x,y}) = 1, in both worlds.
  • x ≡ y — a same-link. c({x,y}) ≡ 0 (mod 2).

⊕ is exclusive or, so links compose: x ⊕ y with y ⊕ z gives x ≡ z. Count the ⊕ edges along any path between two cells — odd means they differ, even means they match.

An odd ⊕-cycle is ⊥. The chain cannot be 2-colored, so it cannot be tagged, and the assumption that produced it is false. This is the forced to differ, same color test applied to the written chain.

Write the chain as two columns, α | β. The columns correspond to the two tag colors.

  • Clues take three states, not two. ▢ live · ◐ currently unhelpful · ▣ exhausted. The app's dim is binary, and since clues never expire the middle state is the one worth recording.

  • CHAIN has two columns because a chain has two banks. A third column means the structure is a group, not a chain.

Contradiction and discharge

Running a hypothesis on paper

⊢ derives · ∴ therefore · ⊥ contradiction. Enclose a hypothesis in a box and prefix every line inside it with the assumption's number. On ⊥, strike the box and carry out only the conclusion. A derived line left outside the box invalidates the ledger in the same way a stray scratch tag invalidates the board.

Four common contradiction patterns:

  • c(S) = 1 with {x,y} ⊆ S, x ≡ y, the rest of S innocent → that bank is impossible, so the other color is the criminal bank.
  • An odd ⊕-cycle → ⊥, without case analysis.
  • c(S) > |S|, or a group's quota exceeding its surviving members → ⊥.
  • |Δrow| + |Δcol| + 1 > criminals remaining → the connection is impossible. Count contradictions surface first; test this one before adjacency.

Notation hygiene

Rules, and a worked derivation

  • Do not record what the board already shows. Placements live in the app; the page holds relations, counts, and provenance.
  • Every derived line carries its sources. (4,6) means clues 4 and 6. On a stall, reread by provenance rather than rereading every line.
  • Strike, do not erase. A struck line ending in ⊥ is a result, and it prevents retrying that branch later.
  • One symbol, one meaning, for the whole board. ⊕ never stands for "at least one". A clue that will not fit the notation is a group, not a link.
  • Transcribe first, deduce second. The transcription pass is where "directly above" and "above" are distinguished.

Steps 1–4 of the worked example, in ledger form. Three clues, one ⊥, two placements:

  1. 01(N(C2) ∩ N(B3)) ∩ I = ∅ — Adam
  2. 02N(C2) ∩ N(B3) = {B2, C3} (glossary: excludes C2, B3)
  3. 03∴ {B2, C3} ⊆ C (1,2) — placed, no tag
  4. 04i({A2, B3, A4, B4}) = 1 — Gus; B2 ∈ C already removed
  5. 05C ∩ ↓B1 connected in G₄ — Laura
  6. 06B3 ∈ I ⇒ B4 ∈ I (5) prefix rule, B2 ∈ C
  7. 07B3 ∈ I ⊢ i({A2,B3,A4,B4}) ≥ 2 ⊥ (4,6)

Line 6 carries the board's key step. Connectivity along one line is an implication, and once written as one, combining it with the count on line 4 takes a single step.

Advanced / Procedure

Complement counting

Track criminals and innocents on all nine lines

Track both criminals and innocents on all nine lines. A column holding exactly 3 criminals also holds exactly 2 innocents, and the second fact is frequently the one that applies first.

  • Keep a running placed / capacity tally per line. Rows cap at 4, columns at 5.
  • A row needing 2 criminals with an "exactly 1" group covering 3 of its 4 cells forces the fourth cell outright.
  • Any group fully contained in another group of the same count makes every cell outside the inner one innocent.
  • Check for branch-independent facts. If a line's only unresolved cells are two chain cells of opposite colors, that line's count is fixed under both assignments. Record it.

Case analysis

Proof by contradiction

Assume a cell is criminal, propagate the consequences, and look for a contradiction. A contradiction proves the cell innocent: this is a deduction, not a guess.

  1. Choose the branch point by consequences: the cell in the most clues, or on the tightest count line.
  2. Assume criminal rather than innocent. Most clues count criminals, so that assumption propagates further.
  3. Mark everything in the scratch color only.
  4. Test the connector-cost floor first; count contradictions surface faster than adjacency ones.
  5. Contradiction → place the opposite and clear scratch. No contradiction → clear scratch, pick a different branch point.

When the board stalls

Work this list in order before branching

  1. Re-read the clue you last dimmed. Clues never expire, and new placements make old clues binding again.
  2. Scan the tagged cells. Any resolution there cascades through the whole chain.
  3. Subtract every pair's guaranteed contribution from every live clue that contains one. Most stalls break here.
  4. Re-run the quota check on every live group. A satisfied group yields placements, but only if the quota is rechecked.
  5. Sweep every line's complement count. Look for lines where the innocents, not the criminals, are nearly full.
  6. Re-check each vocabulary term you relied on. Neighbors vs connected, directly above vs above, edges including corners, "all" vs "any".
  7. Check the corners. With three neighbors and two exits, they resolve under constraints that leave the interior untouched.
  8. Only then, case analysis.

One caution: unsupported taps are blocked rather than penalized, so the solver can be probed for answers. Doing so substitutes its reasoning for yours. Treat a blocked tap as evidence that a step is missing, not as a query.

Results from this board

Three general conclusions

  • Seven placements came from unresolved pairs. Phil, Barb, Nancy, Isaac, Maria, Evie, and the Chad/Henry pair itself all followed from a differ-pair's guaranteed contribution. Chain X was not broken until step 13, by which point it had already produced four placements.
  • Every comparative clue yielded to the same method. Compute one neighborhood exactly, substitute a pair's fixed contribution into the other, and solve for the single unknown. Six clues, one technique.
  • Connectivity down a column behaved as an ordering constraint. "All criminals below Barb are connected" placed no one by itself. It converted to "Keith innocent ⇒ Oscar innocent", and combining that implication with a count-1 group produced the third placement.

Colors used at peak: three, which is what the corner tap offers before wrapping. The color budget is a hard limit, not a stylistic preference.
