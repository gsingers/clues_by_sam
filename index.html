The premise everything rests on

Every clue is true — including clues from criminals.

There is no lying layer. This is constraint satisfaction, not interrogation. It follows that a clue never expires: one you used on move 3 is still binding on move 20, and the intended crack on a hard board usually needs an early clue re-read in light of something learned later. Dim a clue only when it is genuinely exhausted, not when it is merely unhelpful right now.

Basics · Rules & geometry

Vocabulary, and where it bites

Misreads outnumber missed deductions

neighbors
All 8 surrounding cells, diagonals included.
connected
A chain of orthogonal adjacency. Along a single row or column it means no innocents between any two criminals in that line. A group of one counts as connected.
Two graphs on one board. Diagonal cells are neighbors but are never directly connected.
above / below
Anywhere in the same column. left / right: anywhere in the same row.
directly above
The one immediately adjacent cell — not the whole line.
One word turns a line clue into an adjacency clue. Check for "directly" every time.
edges
Includes the corners — 14 of 20 cells. Only B2–B4 and C2–C4 are interior.
An edge clue is nearly board-wide. Its power comes from how much is already placed.
in between
Strictly between — excludes the two named people.
all X are Y
Carries existential import — also asserts at least one X exists.
both
Exactly 2. any is conditional and promises no existence.
common neighbors
Neighbors of both — excludes the two referenced people themselves.
share
"Have in common." in total means the sum across the named groups.
the most
Always uniquely the most — nobody ties.
more than
Does not require the lesser side to have any at all.
if A then B
Not a biconditional. It does give you the contrapositive: not B → not A.

Two graphs, one board

Switch modes on the word "connected"

Counting clues use the left graph. Connectivity clues use the right one. When you read connected, stop counting and start thinking in blobs and walls.

Board geometry worth memorizing

The grid is only 4 wide — structure does real work

  • A neighbor-count clue points at its own region: high counts force the B/C interior, low counts force corners and edges.
  • Corners are the most constrained cells on the board — 3 neighbors, 2 ways out. Corner clues collapse to pairs fastest.
  • Columns A and D are entirely edge cells. Rows hold 4, columns hold 5 — the same count clue is tighter on a row.
Advanced · Connectivity

The connectivity toolkit

The only global clue type in the game

  • Connectivity down a single line is an ordering constraint, not a count. It converts to implications: if the third cell is innocent, every cell past it is innocent too. Collide that with any count clue on the same cells.
  • Elbows are "at least one," never "exactly one." Keep them out of Bank 1.
  • A not connected clue is an instruction to hunt for the separating wall. At 4 cells wide, walls are cheap.
  • Distance sets a floor on headcount. Connecting two cells takes at least |Δrow| + |Δcol| + 1 cells of that status.

Advanced · The tagging system

Three banks

Tags record relations, not reminders

The board records placements. Tags exist to record structure you've derived but can't place yet. Treat the palette as a scarce register with assigned roles — never let two meanings share a color at once.

Bank 1 · standing

The chain

Cells joined by same- and differ-links. However many cells it holds, a chain always has exactly two banks — so exactly two colors. Either color 1 is the criminal set and color 2 the innocent set, or the reverse.

Bank 2 · transient

Group colors

One color marks one exclusivity group with a known count. The value is seeing groups overlap — containment, arithmetic remainder, and overlap squeeze are invisible in prose and obvious in color. Retire the color the instant the group resolves.

Bank 3 · scratch

Hypotheses only

One color, always the same one, wiped completely before you resume. A speculative mark that survives into your standing banks quietly poisons every later deduction — and you won't be able to find where.

Palette reality: the corner tap cycles only three colors before wrapping to none. More need a long-press, and the card's second corner is a separate slot. Budget accordingly.

Same-links are the half people miss. Two cells forced to share a status take the same color and extend the chain exactly as well as a differ-link does.

Comparative clues

"Equal number of…" · "more … than …"

These are neither counts nor links — they constrain a difference between two neighborhoods, and they don't get a color. Three ways to get purchase:

  1. Cancel the overlap. Shared neighbors contribute equally to both sides and drop out. If each exclusive side shrinks to one cell, "equal" becomes a same-link.
  2. Compute one side exactly, feed a pair into the other. The workhorse. A neighborhood containing a differ-pair has a fixed count contribution from it, so the clue collapses to one unknown.
  3. Chain the equalities. Equality is transitive: several "equal" clues fuse into one equivalence class of counts. Pin any member and you pin all of them.

Bound the small side too — a comparative against a corner (3 neighbors) is far tighter than one against an interior cell (8).

Branch-independent contribution

The single highest-yield move in the game

A differ-pair contributes exactly one criminal and exactly one innocent, in both worlds. A same-pair contributes zero or two — always even. Neither needs resolving to be used.

So whenever a pair sits inside a clue's set, subtract its guaranteed contribution and read what's left:

  • Parity clues — a differ-pair flips the required parity of the remainder; a same-pair leaves it untouched.
  • Comparative clues — a pair fixes one side's count outright, leaving a single unknown.
  • Exact counts — a pair consumes exactly one of the quota.

The value of a pair is what it does while unresolved, not what happens when it collapses. Don't wait to break a chain — spend it.

Before you spend a color

Translate · prune · quota

  1. 01

    Translate the property into cells

    "Exactly one X has a criminal directly above them" names a category, but the constrained cells are elsewhere. Rewrite it as "exactly one criminal among {the cell directly above each X}". Tag those cells — never the category members.

  2. 02

    Prune undefined members

    Drop anyone for whom the relation doesn't exist — nobody in row 1 has a cell above them, nobody in row 5 has one below. Phantom members mis-size the group and corrupt the count.

  3. 03

    Check the quota

    Split into resolved and unresolved, and count what the resolved part already supplies. Only one of the three outcomes below needs a color.

A satisfied group isn't something to track — it's a batch of placements waiting to be collected.

When a chain pays

Check these after every placement

  • Resolve one, resolve all. Prove any single tagged cell and the whole chain collapses in one pass.
  • A bank that can't supply the count → contradiction. If a clue needs one criminal from a set holding two same-colored cells, that world is dead and the other color is the criminal bank.
  • Count collision. A line known to hold zero criminals containing a color-1 cell makes color 1 the innocent bank everywhere.
  • Forced to differ, same color (or forced to match, opposite colors) → that world is impossible. No case analysis required.

Untagged, being stuck means re-deriving the board. Tagged, it means looking at the colored cells first.

Tag hygiene

Where clean solves are won and lost

  • Colors count banks, not cells. Three colors on three chained cells throws away the thing the chain encodes. Merge them to two.
  • Don't tag what a line already shows. A count over a whole row or column needs no color. Colors are for scattered constraints.
  • Don't tag a link that pays immediately. If a same-link resolves in the same breath that created it, it never needed a color.
  • A cell in two structures takes its Bank 1 color, with the group membership noted in the margin.
  • Write down what each color means as you assign it. An unlabeled color is worse than none.

The life cycle. Groups shrink into pairs; pairs fuse into a chain; the chain collapses on a single placement. Retire a color at every step — a stale tag is the main source of late-puzzle confusion.

Run this scan after every placement:

  1. Did it shrink a parity remainder to two cells? → new Bank 1 link.
  2. Did it remove a member from a Bank 2 group? → recheck the quota; the group may now be a pair, or fully satisfied.
  3. Did it resolve a chain cell? → the chain collapses; clear both colors.
  4. Is any color still sitting on a resolved cell? → clear it now.

Step 4 is the one people skip.

Advanced · Notation & the paper ledger

The board as a structure

A handful of symbols carries every clue in the game

The app records placements. Tags record relations you can point at on the board. Everything else — quantifiers, implications, running counts, dead branches — needs a page beside you, and it only pays if the notation is short enough to write faster than you think.

V · C · I
The 20 cells and the two sets that partition them: C ⊎ I = V. A status is written B3 ∈ C.
c(S)
Criminals in the set S. The workhorse. Nearly every clue in the game is an assertion about c of some set — and the hard part of a clue is almost always which set.
i(S)
|S| − c(S). Writing the second line every time is complement counting made mechanical instead of remembered.
N(x) · N₄(x)
The 8-neighborhood, and the orthogonal one. G₄ is the graph the word connected lives in.
x↑ x↓ x← x→
The one cell directly adjacent that way. Undefined at the border — A1↑ does not exist, which is different from being innocent.
↑x ↓x ←x →x
The whole line beyond x. ↓B1 = {B2,B3,B4,B5}.
Which side the arrow sits on is the entire difference between the game's two most-confused clue types. Get it right while transcribing and the misread can't reach the deduction.
R1…R5 · A…D
The nine lines, for the count ledger. Rows cap at 4, columns at 5.

Typing rather than writing? <= for ⊆, & for ∩, + for ⊕, = for ≡, -> for ⇒, !! for ⊥, all and some for ∀ and ∃. c(S) stays exactly as it is.

Pairs are equations

The written half of Bank 1

A tag color is a 2-coloring of a graph. The page records the edges that force it:

  • x ⊕ y — a differ-link. Exactly one of them is criminal: c({x,y}) = 1, in both worlds.
  • x ≡ y — a same-link. c({x,y}) ≡ 0 (mod 2).

⊕ is worth the keystroke because it is exclusive or, so links compose without thought: x ⊕ y with y ⊕ z gives x ≡ z. Count the ⊕ edges along any path between two cells — odd means they differ, even means they match.

An odd ⊕-cycle is ⊥. The chain can't be 2-colored, so it can't be tagged either, and whatever assumption produced it is dead. That's forced to differ, same color turned into something you can see rather than recall.

Write the chain as two columns, α | β. Those columns are the two tag colors: the page says which cells, the board says where.

  • Clues get three states, not two. ▢ live · ◐ quiet for now, reread at the first stall · ▣ genuinely exhausted. The app's dim is binary; clues never expire, so the middle mark is the one that matters.
  • LINES is complement counting with a home. Both columns, all nine lines, updated on every placement.
  • GROUPS is the color legend the tag rules demand you write down. Color, set, count — retire the row and the color together.
  • CHAIN is two columns because a chain has two banks. If you ever need a third column, you've recorded a group, not a chain.

Contradiction and discharge

Paper's version of the scratch color

⊢ derives · ∴ therefore · ⊥ dead. Fence a hypothesis in a box, prefix every line inside it with the assumption's number, and on ⊥ strike the box and lift out only the conclusion. A derived line that escapes the fence poisons the ledger exactly the way a stray scratch tag poisons the board — and is just as hard to find later.

Four signatures worth recognizing on sight:

  • c(S) = 1 with {x,y} ⊆ S, x ≡ y, the rest of S innocent → that bank is dead, so the other color is the criminal one.
  • An odd ⊕-cycle → ⊥ with no case analysis at all.
  • c(S) > |S|, or a group's quota exceeding its surviving members → ⊥.
  • |Δrow| + |Δcol| + 1 > criminals remaining → the connection is impossible. Count contradictions surface first; test this one before adjacency.

Notation hygiene

Plus the same board, worked on paper

  • Never write what the board already shows. Placements live in the app. The page holds relations, counts and provenance — nothing you could read off the grid.
  • Every derived line carries its sources. (4,6) means clues 4 and 6. When you stall, reread by provenance instead of rereading everything.
  • Strike, don't erase. A struck line ending in ⊥ is a result — it's the reason you won't retry that branch at move 20.
  • One symbol, one meaning, all board. ⊕ never drifts into "at least one." If a clue won't sit in the notation, it's a group, not a link.
  • Transcribe first, deduce second. The transcription pass is where "directly above" and "above" separate, and it costs a minute.

Steps 1–4 of the worked example, in ledger form. Three clues, one ⊥, two placements:

  1. 01(N(C2) ∩ N(B3)) ∩ I = ∅ — Adam
  2. 02N(C2) ∩ N(B3) = {B2, C3} (glossary: excludes C2, B3)
  3. 03∴ {B2, C3} ⊆ C (1,2) — placed, no tag
  4. 04i({A2, B3, A4, B4}) = 1 — Gus; B2 ∈ C already removed
  5. 05C ∩ ↓B1 connected in G₄ — Laura
  6. 06B3 ∈ I ⇒ B4 ∈ I (5) prefix rule, B2 ∈ C
  7. 07B3 ∈ I ⊢ i({A2,B3,A4,B4}) ≥ 2 ⊥ (4,6)
  8. 08∴ B3 ∈ C (7)

Line 6 is the whole trick of that board, and in prose it took a paragraph. Connectivity down one line is an implication — once it's written as one, colliding it with the count on line 4 is a single step.

Advanced · Procedure

Counting, done fully

Complement counting closes more Evil boards than cleverness

Track both criminals and innocents for all nine lines. If a column holds exactly 3 criminals it also holds exactly 2 innocents — and the second fact fires first surprisingly often.

  • Keep a running placed / capacity tally per line. Rows cap at 4, columns at 5.
  • A row needing 2 criminals with an "exactly 1" group covering 3 of its 4 cells forces the fourth cell outright.
  • Any group fully contained in another group of the same count makes every cell outside the inner one innocent.
  • Watch for branch-independent facts. If a line's only unresolved cells are two chain cells of opposite colors, that line's count is fixed in both worlds — bank it now.

Case analysis

For when the board genuinely stalls

Assume a cell is criminal, propagate hard, hunt for a contradiction. Find one and that cell is provably innocent — a deduction, not a guess.

  1. Choose the branch point by consequences: the cell in the most clues, or on the tightest count line.
  2. Assume criminal rather than innocent — most clues count criminals, so it propagates harder.
  3. Mark everything in the scratch color only.
  4. Test the connector-cost floor first. Count contradictions surface faster than adjacency ones.
  5. Contradiction → place the opposite and clear scratch. No contradiction → clear scratch, pick a different branch point.

Stuck? Run this order

Breadth beats depth — the crack is rarely where you're staring

  1. Re-read the clue you last dimmed. Clues never expire; new placements give old clues new teeth.
  2. Scan tagged cells. Cheapest possible cascade, and the reason tags exist.
  3. Subtract every pair's guaranteed contribution from every live clue that contains one. This is where most stalls break.
  4. Re-run the quota check on every live group. Satisfied groups turn into free placements and you won't notice unless you look.
  5. Sweep every line's complement count. Look for lines where the innocents, not the criminals, are nearly full.
  6. Re-check each vocabulary term you relied on. Neighbors vs connected, directly above vs above, edges including corners, "all" vs "any".
  7. Look at the corners. Three neighbors and two exits — they resolve under pressure that leaves the interior untouched.
  8. Only now, case analysis.

One standing caution: unsupported taps are blocked rather than penalized, so it's possible to probe the solver for answers. It works, and it substitutes its reasoning for yours — a blocked tap is best read as a signal you jumped ahead, not as a free query.

What this board taught

Three things worth carrying forward

  • Seven placements came from pairs nobody resolved. Phil, Barb, Nancy, Isaac, Maria, Evie, and the Chad/Henry pair itself all fell out of a differ-pair's guaranteed contribution. Chain X wasn't broken until step 13, by which point it had already paid four times.
  • Every comparative fell to the same move. Compute one neighborhood exactly, feed a pair's fixed contribution into the other, solve for the single unknown. Six clues, one technique.
  • Connectivity down a column behaved as an ordering constraint. "All criminals below Barb are connected" never placed anyone by itself — it converted to "Keith innocent ⇒ Oscar innocent," and colliding that implication with a count-1 group is what produced the third placement.

Colors used at peak: three — which is exactly what the corner tap offers before it wraps. The ranking discipline isn't theoretical; the UI enforces it.
